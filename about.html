About

Origin

It started with the code.

This website presents my submission for the 2026 British Physics Olympiad Computational Challenge, centred on the theme of quantum physics.

The project brings together ten computational tasks exploring how mathematical models, numerical methods, and interactive simulations can be used to investigate physical phenomena that are often difficult to observe directly.

The original scientific models were written in Python. This website turns their results into a browser-based experience so that the tasks, explanations, and visual evidence can be explored without specialist software.

Method

The physics comes first.

Each task begins with the relevant physical principles and mathematical relationships. The models are tested against analytical results where possible and examined across a range of parameters.

The project distinguishes established physics, numerical approximations, and exploratory extensions. Assumptions and limitations are recorded alongside the results, with validation checks used where appropriate.

The aim is not simply to produce convincing animations, but to create models whose behaviour can be explained, tested, and justified.

Ten

Ten problems. One thread.

The challenge moves from statistical motion and thermal radiation to quantum systems and modern applications. Each task combines theory, a computational model, numerical results, and an explanation of its assumptions and limitations.

Seeing

Make the invisible move.

Quantum physics is frequently presented through dense notation and static diagrams.

Animation, responsive controls, and data visualisation help visitors observe relationships that may be less obvious from equations alone. The visual language draws inspiration from wavefunctions, interference patterns, probability fields, and atomic structure.

Clarity remains more important than spectacle: every visual element should help communicate the physics or make the project easier to navigate.
